Using the Polar H10 on your horse: how to measure resting HRV correctly

With the Polar H10 in the equine strap, you record the intervals between your horse's heartbeats at rest. Resting heart rate variability (HRV) is calculated from these. This lets your vet practice see, ahead of the visit, whether your horse's resting readings have changed. For two measurements to be comparable, what matters is good electrode contact and consistent conditions.

What you need

  • A Polar H10 sensor in the Polar equine strap, available from specialist retailers.
  • Water or electrode gel for the electrodes. On a horse, gel gives the cleaner signal because the coat increases resistance.
  • An Android smartphone or a computer with Chrome or Edge for the free measuring station in the browser. For iPhone, the CARLON Equine app is in development, as Apple does not allow Bluetooth in the browser.
  • About 15 minutes of your time, including at least seven minutes of undisturbed recording.

Step-by-step guide

  1. Choose a quiet settingMeasure in the horse's usual stable, at the usual time of day, at least two hours after the horse has been worked (our own rule of thumb for comparable readings) and not while it is feeding. This keeps later measurements comparable.
  2. Moisten the electrodes thoroughlyMoisten the electrode surfaces of the strap, ideally with electrode gel. A dry strap often causes the signal to drop out.
  3. Fit the strapFit the equine strap as described in the strap's instructions and click the H10 into place.
  4. Open the measuring station and connectOpen the measuring station in the browser, or, once it is available, the iPhone app, enter the horse's name and connect the H10.
  5. Record at rest for at least seven minutesKeep the horse standing quietly, keep your device nearby and leave the screen on. The station shows at all times whether the signal is good enough to analyse.
  6. Send the recording to the practiceWhen you have finished, tap ‘Send’ and send the file however you normally contact your practice, for example by text message or email. If your practice uses CARLON Equine Visite, it compares the recording with your horse's earlier resting values.

Tip for Android: use the Chrome menu ⋮ and ‘Add to Home screen’ to add the measuring station to your phone like an app. You can then open it with a single tap. You can also measure at the stables with no mobile signal; you will need a connection again to send the recording.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Dry electrodes: The signal drops out or is unstable. Moisten again, preferably with gel.
  • Disturbances during recording: Feeding, movement or visitors will affect the readings. It is best to repeat the measurement later.
  • Inconsistent conditions: An unfamiliar stable, a different time of day or measuring shortly after exercise can all make recordings hard to compare.
  • Recordings that are too short: Values from short recordings vary more. The station recommends at least seven minutes; this is the minimum we have set for comparable trends.
  • Device too far away or screen off: The Bluetooth connection may drop. The station reconnects automatically, and the beats recorded up to that point are retained.
Longer pauses between heartbeats do occur in resting horses, often owing to second-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (Eggensperger & Schwarzwald 2017). The analysis reports them separately so that they do not skew the results. What a pause means is for your vet to assess.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a Polar Equine app for the iPhone?

According to its own support page, Polar offers the Polar Equine app for Android phones. For the iPhone, we are preparing the CARLON Equine app, which records using the Polar H10 in the equine strap.

Can I use the Polar H10 in a browser?

Yes, on Android devices and on a computer with Chrome or Edge. The CARLON measuring station runs there free of charge in the browser, with no app and no account. On iPhone and iPad, Apple does not allow Bluetooth in the browser.

What does resting HRV tell you?

In horses, a single value tells you little, because there is no generally applicable reference range for recordings made in the stable. It becomes meaningful when compared with earlier measurements of the same horse under the same conditions.

Is the measurement a diagnosis?

No. The measurement provides the intervals between heartbeats and the metrics calculated from them. Clinical assessment is a matter for your vet alone. If your horse shows signs of acute illness, call your practice.

Who receives my recording?

Only the practice you send it to. The measuring station creates the file on your device, and you pass it on yourself.
